The case

Device to portal, and back. The brief's constraints.

  • ~50,000 devices, ~400 tenants — and 8,000 of them at a single site.
  • Firmware is another team's. 12–18 months to reach a meaningful share, never all of it.
  • So: many firmware versions on the wire at once, permanently.
  • Devices go quiet for hours or months, then reconnect with a backlog to dump.
  • Users still expect current state, and to act — restart, push config, open a session.

Disclaimers

Where I have interpreted the brief, and what this deliberately is not.

  • I have taken a complete design as the goal. Normally I would keep most of this high-level and deep-dive piece by piece — through consultation, exploration and spikes with the people who own each constraint.
  • I deliver what was asked. I would not normally fix ownership and delegation up front, and I would not write a delivery plan for its own sake — only for contingency and mitigation.
  • The design is AI-first. The initial shape is mine; from there I let it overrule me on some things and held my ground on others. Both are logged, with what each acceptance cost.
  • The plan excludes process. No team formation, project setup or delay budget. It assumes an autonomous cross-functional team, and a shared testing framework that exists or can be enabled.
  • The two hours were not measured as instructed. With AI in the loop, focused work is not meaningfully measurable — and time between commits is not time spent on this problem, it is usually time spent on another.

What I had to assume

The brief describes the fleet. It does not describe how a device behaves — so five assumptions carry this design.

  • No device is reachable from outside. Every exchange starts with the device — so a command is something we leave out to be collected, never a call we make.
  • Clocks drift, and reset to epoch on power loss. So we order records by a number we stamp on arrival, and treat the device's own timestamp as display material.
  • A device clears its flash when we answer 200. So that answer promises durability, not understanding — and our log becomes the only copy.
  • Reconnecting devices re-send buffered records with a stable id. So de-duplication is a high-water mark rather than content hashing.
  • Commands are a human pressing a button on one device — order 8,000 a day across 50,000 devices.

That last one is why this is not a distributed-data problem. At this size, designing as though it were would be the most expensive mistake.

Five principles I designed against

Each one names its own failure test. The weaknesses slide grades the design against them.

  1. No cloud capability may require a firmware change to function. "Requires firmware ≥ X" is a failure.
  2. Firmware version is visible only to the edge parsers. One version conditional downstream is a failure.
  3. The cloud never initiates contact with a device. A cloud→device arrow that is not a response is a failure.
  4. No value reaches a user without its age. A response schema with a bare value is a failure.
  5. One tenant's burst cannot degrade another's. A shared unbounded resource on the hot path is a failure.

Ingest

  • Save it, answer the device, work out what it means afterwards.
  • The hard part is not volume — it is 8,000 devices reconnecting at once.
  • A record we cannot read is set aside; the rest keep flowing.
  • Firmware we have never seen is read as far as we can, never refused.
  • Big customers get a proportional share, not an equal one.

ADR-003The ack means received and durable, not understood.

ADR-004Per-tenant log partitions for isolation, device-count-weighted admission for fairness.

ADR-005Quarantine the poison record with its raw bytes and continue.

State and freshness

  • We order records by arrival, because device clocks cannot be trusted.
  • Freshness is worked out when you look, not kept up to date by a job.
  • Two separate questions: is the device talking, and how old is this value?
  • A bad clock still measures gaps correctly, so we use it for that only.
  • Some ages are estimates, and we say which ones.
expected_interval = median(last 20 live inter-arrival gaps), capped at 24 h stale_after = last_contact_at + max(2 × expected_interval, 15 min) offline_after = last_contact_at + max(6 × expected_interval, 45 min)

ADR-006Order per field on cloud-assigned ingest_seq; observed_at is display material only.

ADR-007Derive the freshness class at read; materialise the boundaries, never the class.

ADR-008Never-reported and gone-quiet are separate classes; class per device, age per field.

Commands

  • We record the request and watch it. We never claim the device acted.
  • Being offline does not block the request — it changes what we tell you.
  • Requests expire, so an old one cannot surprise someone months later.
  • "We do not know" is shown with the evidence behind it.
  • A remote session needs a live device, so it is not queued like the rest.

ADR-009The 202 promises recorded and watchable, never that the device will act.

ADR-012A three-rung ladder — Confirmed, Presumed executed, Unknown — each shown with its basis. Unknown is terminal.

ADR-010TTL is wall-clock, per command type. ADR-011Idempotence belongs to the command type. ADR-013The session is a relay, not a queued command.

What this design does badly

  • Nine named costs. Each has someone who feels it and a trigger to rethink.
  • Two of my own five principles do not hold. One fails outright.
  • Some ages are estimates that can read younger than the truth.
  • On old firmware, "did it work?" is usually a hedge.
  • During our own outage, the portal blames 400 customers' fleets.

Principle 2Fail — firmware version does leak past the parsers (RSK-008). Principle 5 is a partial pass; 4 passes on the letter, not the spirit.

Eight weeks

  • Only one thing here cannot be redone later: keeping the data.
  • Everything else we can rebuild by replaying the log.
  • So we ship the whole path shallow, not one part deep.
  • We feed the new system from the old one, without touching devices.
  • Week eight proves the path works. It does not prove it scales.

Who owns what

  • Each engineer owns one area end to end, not a layer of everything.
  • The senior takes the part we cannot undo later.
  • The newest person gets the old firmware — bounded, testable, and it teaches the domain.
  • I keep the contracts between areas, and what the portal tells users.
  • Keeping the most interesting problem for myself would be the classic mistake.

DEL-010The canonical observation contract. DEL-011The ack semantics. DEL-012What the portal says.

DEL-013The firmware relationship — including refusing "just require firmware ≥ X". DEL-014Which weaknesses we accept.

The one firmware ask

  • Anything firmware ships takes years and never reaches every device.
  • So the ask can never be something we are waiting on.
  • I ask for one thing: send back the request id we handed you.
  • It tells us what actually happened, instead of us guessing.
  • We build the guess anyway, and we keep it forever.

DEL-020Echo the intent_id on the next sync — one opaque string, stored and returned.

DEL-021The corroboration ladder we ship regardless. DEL-022Capability inferred from evidence, so a device that echoes upgrades itself silently.
